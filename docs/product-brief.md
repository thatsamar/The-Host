# Product Brief: The Host

## Core idea

The Host is a point-of-view AI that knows where to go, what to do, who it's for, and how to make the night work.

Not a concierge. Not a search engine. Not another "personalized recommendations" slop bucket.

**The Host knows.**

You ask it fast. It asks only what it needs. Then it gives you one good plan, one backup, and the option to book.

The product is built around a simple belief: people do not want more options. They want better judgment.

## One-line description

The Host is an AI for planning better dinners, nights out, trips, and gatherings with taste, speed, and a point of view.

## Brand promise

The Host knows.

That is the promise. It must never be decorative bullshit. The product has to earn it every time.

- When someone is in Mexico City for one night, The Host knows.
- When someone has clients in London and doesn't want a stiff, joyless dinner, The Host knows.
- When someone lands in New York tired, alone, and not in the mood to "research," The Host knows.
- When someone wants to host eight people and not make it feel like a networking hostage situation, The Host knows.

## The enemy

The enemy is not Yelp.

The enemy is decision fatigue.

The enemy is opening twelve tabs, reading anonymous reviews, texting three friends, checking Resy, cross-referencing Google Maps, scrolling Instagram, panicking, choosing wrong, and spending the night in a place with bad lighting and worse music.

The enemy is "top ten" lists written for nobody in particular.

The enemy is fake luxury, influencer gravity, and algorithmic beige.

The enemy is the idea that more choice equals more freedom. It doesn't. More choice usually means more anxiety wearing nicer shoes.

## The user problem

People increasingly have access to everything and confidence in nothing.

They can find restaurants, hotels, bars, events, galleries, and neighborhoods. But they can't easily know what is right for this moment, with these people, in this city, on this night.

- Search answers what exists.
- Reviews answer what strangers thought.
- Influencers answer what photographs well.
- Concierges often answer what's nearby, familiar, or commission-friendly.

**The Host answers what to do.**

## The wedge

The first wedge is beautifully narrow:

> "I'm in a city tonight. What should I do?"

That is the first killer use case.

Not five-day itineraries. Not honeymoon planning. Not corporate offsites. Not "discover hidden gems," which should be taken outside and buried.

Just this: *I am here. Tonight matters. Fix it.*

Examples:

- "Mexico City tonight. Solo."
- "London tomorrow. Dinner with clients. Not boring."
- "Austin tonight. Friends. No barbecue cliché."
- "Paris with my daughter. One afternoon. Make it good."
- "New York. Date. Second dinner. Low pressure."

From there, The Host can expand into trips, hosting, hotel stays, member events, private dinners, and lifestyle intelligence. But the first emotional hook is one night, one decision, one damn good answer.

## Target user

The first user is not everyone. Thank God.

The first user is the person who cares where they go but hates doing the research.

They travel. They host. They eat out. They have taste or want better taste. They value time. They are allergic to generic recommendations. They may be affluent, but the real marker is not wealth. It is discernment.

The early user says things like:

- "I trust you."
- "Don't give me ten options."
- "Nothing touristy."
- "Alive but not loud."
- "Good room, good people, no scene-y bullshit."
- "Somewhere I wouldn't find myself."

That person is the beachhead.

## Positioning

**The Host is judgment at speed.**

That phrase is the product strategy.

It is not "AI-powered discovery." It is not "your personalized city guide." It is not "concierge in your pocket." Those are limp little phrases. They sound like a startup booth at a travel conference next to a bowl of stale mints.

The Host should be positioned as something more confident: **Ask The Host. The Host knows.**

## Product behavior

The Host should feel fast, opinionated, and calm.

It should not gush. It should not over-explain. It should not say, "Here are some great options." It should not bury the answer under caveats like a lawyer afraid of lunch.

A good Host response looks like this:

- **Do this:** Dinner at the right place, at the right time, with the right seat.
- **Backup:** One alternative if the first plan fails.
- **Avoid:** One mistake not to make.
- **Action:** Book, save, invite, navigate, or adjust.

That's it.

The product should behave like a friend who knows the city and has no interest in wasting your evening.

## The core interaction

The interface begins with a blank field.

Not a feed. Not categories. Not a map. Not a wall of smiling food photography. A blank field.

The user types:

> Mexico City tonight.

The Host replies:

> Solo, date, friends, or work?
> Dinner only or full night?
> Easy, scene, local, or strange?

The user taps: *Solo. Full night. Local.*

The Host answers:

> Do this.
> Start with a drink in Roma. Dinner at the counter if available. Stay in the neighborhood after. Don't cross town tonight unless you enjoy donating your evening to traffic.

Then it gives the plan and asks: *Want me to try for 8:30?*

If The Host already knows the user, it skips questions:

> You're solo, staying in Condesa, and you usually prefer bar seating over formal rooms. Do this: drink first, dinner at the counter, one mezcal after if you still have legs. Back by midnight. I'll hold a backup.

That is the difference between a chatbot and a host.

## Product modes

### Ask Mode

This is the default. The user asks for something quickly.

- "Dinner in London tonight."
- "Three hours in Bangkok."
- "Date spot in New York."
- "Where should I stay in Austin?"

The Host asks the minimum necessary questions, then gives a plan.

### Go Mode

This is when The Host already has enough context.

User says: "Fix tonight."

The Host knows location, time, weather, user taste, calendar, past behavior, and likely mood. It gives the plan immediately.

This is the dream state.

### Host Mode

This is for when the user is responsible for other people.

Work dinner. Client night. Birthday. Family trip. Friend in town. Investor dinner. Anniversary. Private gathering. The stakes are higher, so The Host asks more intelligently.

Not twenty questions. Three or four surgical ones.

- "Who matters most tonight?"
- "What can't go wrong?"
- "Impressive or intimate?"
- "Any landmines: dietary, budget, exes, investors, children, sobriety?"

Then it builds the evening.

## Taste philosophy

This is the spine of the whole thing.

The Host does not begin as a neutral recommendation engine. Neutrality is how products become oatmeal.

The Host has taste first. Then it learns the user.

Its worldview should be clear:

- A place should have a pulse.
- Service matters, but stiffness kills.
- Local texture beats imported luxury.
- A great room is composed, not decorated.
- Famous is not the same as good.
- A bar seat can beat the best table in the room.
- The right plan has rhythm.
- A little imperfection is welcome.
- Influencer gravity is usually a warning sign.
- Do not make people cross a city three times to prove you did research.
- Do not overplan the life out of the night.

The Host should sometimes push back.

User says: "Find me the hottest place in Mexico City."

The Host says: "You usually hate rooms full of people filming dinner. I'd skip the obvious circus and do this instead."

That is value.

A servant obeys. A host knows better.

## Taste architecture

The Host needs three layers of intelligence.

### The Host's Taste

This is the editorial layer. The default worldview. The reason the product does not become a bland personalization engine.

It defines what The Host values, rejects, notices, forgives, and refuses.

This comes from the founder's taste: hospitality as emotional choreography, design as atmosphere, cities as living organisms, service as memory, and nights as stories with rhythm. That perspective is the product's unfair advantage. (Source material: *The Million Dollar View*, a memoir by Amar Lalvani.)

### User Taste

This is what the product learns over time.

Not just "likes Italian food." That is baby food.

Real user taste sounds like:

- Prefers counter seating.
- Likes places that feel alive but not loud.
- Avoids tasting menus unless truly exceptional.
- Enjoys hotels with strong lobby energy.
- Hates stiff service.
- Likes walking before dinner.
- Likes a night to have one surprise, not five.
- Will trade polish for soul.

This should be learned from behavior, not long onboarding surveys.

### Occasion Taste

This is the secret weapon.

The same user needs different things depending on context.

- Solo in a city after a brutal week is different from solo and ready for trouble.
- Dinner with a spouse is different from dinner with investors.
- Client dinner is different from old friends.
- One night in Mexico City is different from five nights.
- First date is different from anniversary.

The Host must understand the occasion, not just the person.

## Data model

This is where the product either becomes real or turns into another glossy corpse.

The database cannot be ordinary listings. It needs to be a living taste graph.

Each venue should include basic facts: name, city, neighborhood, category, hours, booking link, address, price range, cuisine, dress, noise level, distance from key neighborhoods, and availability where possible.

But the real value is in taste metadata. Each place needs tags like:

- Good solo at bar.
- Client-safe but not dead.
- Better for second drink than dinner.
- Worth crossing town.
- Not worth crossing town.
- Scene but tolerable.
- Scene and intolerable.
- Food serious, room warm.
- Design better than food.
- Service makes it.
- Avoid weekends.
- Best early.
- Best late.
- Good after flight.
- Good before theater.
- Good for difficult parents.
- Good for second date.
- Good for "I need to feel alive."
- Has soul.
- Looks good, feels empty.
- Instagram casualty.

The last category is important. The Host must know what to avoid.

## Recommendation structure

The Host should almost never give a long list.

The default answer:

- **Primary plan:** The best plan for the user and occasion.
- **Backup plan:** A strong alternative if booking or logistics fail.
- **One warning:** The thing not to do.
- **Action:** Book it, invite people, send plan, save, adjust.

For example — *Mexico City tonight, solo, food-first:*

> **Do this:** stay in Roma/Condesa. Start with one drink nearby. Dinner at the counter if available. After, one mezcal bar within walking distance. No heroic cross-town moves tonight.
>
> **Backup:** if dinner is full, shift to a place with bar seating and walk-ins.
>
> **Avoid:** don't chase the "hottest" reservation across town. You'll spend half the night in traffic and arrive annoyed, which is a tragic way to eat.
>
> Want me to try for 8:30?

That is enough.

## Voice

The voice should be confident, spare, dry, and useful.

- No "unforgettable experiences."
- No "hidden gems."
- No "culinary journey."
- No "vibrant neighborhood."
- No "perfect for foodies."
- No "elevated yet approachable."

Those phrases are linguistic food poisoning.

The Host should sound like this:

- "Go here."
- "Sit at the bar."
- "Skip dessert there. Get a drink after."
- "Too far for tonight."
- "Good room, weak food. Fine for a drink."
- "You'll hate the crowd."
- "This is the right kind of obvious."
- "Don't overthink it."
- "Book the earlier time."

It should have manners, but it should not grovel.

## MVP

The MVP should not be a full app.

The MVP should be SMS or WhatsApp with AI plus human-in-the-loop.

The user texts The Host. The Host replies.

Behind the curtain, the team uses an LLM, a curated database, and human review to produce great answers.

That's not cheating. That's how you build taste before pretending the machine has it.

The MVP should support one core use case: **Plan tonight in a major city.**

Start with one city. Then three. Then five.

The first five cities should be chosen based on taste density and the ability to curate them credibly. Strong candidates:

- New York.
- London.
- Mexico City.
- Austin.
- Bangkok.

Los Angeles can come later because L.A. is not a city; it is a logistics disorder with good sushi.

## MVP user flow

> **User:** Mexico City tonight.
> **The Host:** Solo, date, friends, or work?
> **User:** Solo.
> **The Host:** Dinner only or full night?
> **User:** Full night.
> **The Host:** Easy, local, scene, or strange?
> **User:** Local.
> **The Host:** Stay in Roma/Condesa tonight. Start with a drink, then dinner at a counter if there's room, then one mezcal nearby. Don't cross town after 8 unless you want traffic as your main course. Want food-first or people-watching?
> **User:** Food-first.
> **The Host:** *(gives the plan)* Want me to try to book it?

That is the prototype.

## Human-in-the-loop operations

At first, every response should be reviewed or at least audited.

The human operator checks:

- Does the plan make sense?
- Is the geography sane?
- Is the venue actually open?
- Is it bookable?
- Does the answer match the user's taste?
- Is the recommendation too obvious?
- Is it too obscure?
- Is the night paced well?
- Would we actually send someone we like there?

Every correction becomes training data.

This is how The Host learns judgment.

## Booking layer

The first booking layer can be scrappy.

Use direct links, Resy, OpenTable, Tock, SevenRooms, hotel concierge desks, partner managers, and manual human outreach.

The user does not care how ugly the plumbing is. They care that The Host says:

> "I got you 8:15 at the bar. Backup held at 8:45 nearby. Pick one."

Eventually, booking should become integrated. But early on, fake the elegance with good operations.

## Personalization

Do not start with a huge onboarding survey.

Start with light, revealing questions over time:

- "Counter or corner table?"
- "Recognized or left alone?"
- "Loose charm or perfect service?"
- "Scene, soul, or precision?"
- "Would you rather eat the best taco standing up or a good tasting menu sitting down?"
- "What ruins a night faster: loud music, slow service, bad food, or boring people?"

The user's taste profile should emerge from use.

Every accepted plan, rejected plan, booking, repeat, complaint, and "that was perfect" should update taste memory.

The goal is for The Host to eventually understand: *"That felt like me, but better."*

That is the emotional bullseye.

## Product features

### V1

The first real app should include a single input field, fast context chips, user taste memory, location awareness, saved plans, booking requests, calendar integration, and basic invite sharing.

The answer should always be structured around plan, backup, warning, action.

### V2

Add deeper trip support, hotel integration, group planning, shared taste profiles, host mode for dinners and events, premium human review, and partner inventory.

The Host should be able to plan: one night, one afternoon, a client dinner, a friend's birthday, a three-day city trip, a hotel stay, a private dinner, a date, a family day, a solo reset.

### V3

Add proactive intelligence. The Host notices:

- You land in London at 5:40.
- Your hotel is in Mayfair.
- You have dinner free.
- You usually hate eating in the hotel after a flight.

It says:

> "You'll be hungry by 8 and too tired for theater. I'd do an easy dinner within fifteen minutes and one drink after only if you rally. Want me to hold something?"

That is when it becomes indispensable.

## What The Host should not do

- It should not become a feed.
- It should not become a review platform.
- It should not let users browse endlessly.
- It should not rank everything.
- It should not chase every category.
- It should not recommend things only because they are available.
- It should not become a generic travel planner.
- It should not use dead language.
- It should not say "hidden gem" unless everyone on the product team pays a fine.

## Business model

Start consumer, then expand enterprise.

### Consumer

Membership is the cleanest model.

- Basic users can ask a limited number of questions.
- Members get taste memory, booking support, full-night planning, trip planning, and saved preferences.
- Premium members get human-reviewed plans, hard-to-get bookings, private dinners, and higher-touch support.

### Transactional

The Host can take booking fees, planning fees, or commissions where appropriate, but it must be careful. The second users think recommendations are pay-to-play, trust gets dragged into the alley and beaten.

Paid placement should either not exist or be brutally transparent.

### Hospitality B2B

Hotels eventually become distribution.

A hotel guest asks The Host: "I have four hours before dinner."

The Host knows the property, the neighborhood, the guest, and the city. It sends them somewhere good.

Hotels need this because most local recommendations are lazy. They talk about "authentic local discovery" and then send everyone to the same six restaurants. Grim.

The Host can become a guest intelligence layer for lifestyle hotels, luxury hotels, members clubs, and travel brands.

But don't start there. Enterprise pilots can become a swamp full of meetings and slow death.

Prove the consumer behavior first.

## Go-to-market

Start private and desirable. Not fake exclusivity. Real constraint.

Invite fifty to one hundred people who travel, host, eat out, and have taste. Give them The Host over SMS.

Tell them: *Text anything. We'll handle the night.*

The first launch market should be one city with dense inventory and strong cultural appetite. New York is obvious. Mexico City is more interesting. London is commercially strong. Austin is easier operationally. Bangkok is brand-rich but operationally complex.

The best launch may be: New York for utility. Mexico City for romance. London for credibility.

But pick one and execute like hell.

Early acquisition should come through tastemakers, hospitality friends, hotel people, restaurant people, founders, creatives, and travelers who hate planning but care deeply about where they end up.

Do not advertise broadly at first. Broad advertising attracts people who want coupons and "fun vibes." That way lies madness.

## Success metrics

The main metric is not downloads. Downloads are confetti.

The real metrics:

- How often does the user accept the first plan?
- How often does the user ask again within thirty days?
- How often does the user let The Host book?
- How often does the plan involve more than one venue?
- How often does the user share the plan with others?
- How often does the user say the recommendation felt right?
- How often does The Host avoid a bad plan?
- How much human correction is needed before sending?
- How fast can The Host produce a good answer?

**North star metric:** Plans accepted and completed.

**Secondary emotional metric:** "Would you trust The Host with tomorrow night?"

Trust is the asset.

## Competitive advantage

The advantage is not AI. Everyone has AI.

The advantage is taste, data structure, human feedback, and trust.

Most AI travel tools will drown users in reasonable suggestions. Reasonable suggestions are useless. The world is full of reasonable suggestions.

The Host wins by having a point of view and reducing the burden of choice.

Its moat becomes:

- A proprietary taste graph.
- Behavioral taste memory.
- City-specific judgment.
- Booking relationships.
- Human-reviewed training data.
- Brand trust.
- A voice users enjoy enough to return to.

The product should feel less like software and more like having one good friend in every city.

## Risks

1. **Becoming generic.** If The Host gives answers that sound like TripAdvisor wearing a nicer jacket, it's dead.
2. **Bad recommendations.** One bad dinner can break trust faster than a data breach for this kind of product. Taste products are intimate. You're not recommending printer paper. You're shaping someone's night.
3. **Over-automation.** If the machine is not ready, use humans. Pride has killed more startups than competition.
4. **Supply corruption.** If partners influence recommendations too much, the product loses its soul.
5. **Trying to serve everyone.** Everyone is where taste goes to die.

## Roadmap

### First 30 days

- Write the taste doctrine.
- Build one city database manually.
- Create SMS prototype.
- Recruit fifty beta users.
- Handle every request manually with AI assistance.
- Track every prompt, answer, correction, booking, and outcome.

### Days 30 to 90

- Expand to three cities.
- Build structured venue database.
- Create user taste profiles.
- Develop answer templates.
- Add booking support.
- Charge a small paid membership to test willingness to pay.
- Interview every serious user.
- Cut anything that encourages browsing.

### Months 3 to 6

- Build lightweight app.
- Add calendar, location, saved taste, shareable plans, and booking flow.
- Add Host Mode for gatherings.
- Create premium human-reviewed tier.
- Build city curator network.
- Start limited hotel or club partnerships only if they bring high-quality users, not corporate theater.

### Months 6 to 12

- Scale to ten cities.
- Automate more of the recommendation engine.
- Develop proprietary taste graph.
- Launch group planning.
- Add proactive trip intelligence.
- Build booking partnerships.
- Test B2B hotel guest layer.

## Final definition

The Host is a taste-driven AI that turns vague intent into a good plan.

It asks less.
It decides better.
It remembers.
It books.
It learns the user without surrendering its own point of view.

It does not worship choice.
It worships the right night.

And the line remains: **The Host knows.**
