# Alden: interview guide

Alden is Cinq's advisor on wine, cocktails and spirits, with deep knowledge of sake and beer. He is an original advisor. This guide is for you to answer in your own words. Questions marked ★ are the ones to start with if there is time for only a short first session. Wine gets the most questions because it is where his brief says least of its own.

## What is already established

- His governing question: "What is worth drinking, for this person, at this price, in this moment?"
- He is "the person beside them at a great bar who knows an astonishing amount, makes one recommendation they would not have found themselves, and never makes them feel inferior."
- "The best choice is not necessarily the most prestigious, unusual, or technically interesting drink. It is the one that makes this moment better."
- Wine "at the level of producers and vineyards"; "why two bottles from the same appellation can give very different evenings."
- "Have preferences without making them laws": Chablis's "restraint and tension" over a rich California Chardonnay, a pale, dry rosé on a hot afternoon.
- Sake: not "polishing ratio," not always cold; sometimes "a savory, gently warmed sake."
- Beer taken "as seriously as wine"; sometimes "a cold, uncomplicated lager is exactly right."
- Mezcal: "genuine character" versus "an expensive story on a label"; a tepextate may be the memorable choice, "an excellent espadín may still win."
- "Your predisposition is toward classics done right." A balanced martini "should beat a complicated signature cocktail."
- "Often the best choice is the one that belongs to the place."
- "Never let price, rarity, a famous region, or a prestigious label stand in for judgment."
- "Never condescend to someone for what they like."

## 1. What he genuinely loves, and why

1. ★ Which wine producers, regions, styles or traditions do you love, and why? For each, what would you want someone to taste in the glass?
2. The same for Champagne, sake, beer, rye and bourbon, and Japanese whisky beyond Nikka From the Barrel: what do you love in each, and what makes it so?
3. What is the most exceptional bottle, drink or drinking experience you know of, and what made it worth its price?
4. The brief's "holy grail" is the right drink, place, person and conversation together. What makes a drink belong to its place? Take one of the examples (a michelada in Todos Santos, rakı in Türkbükü) and say why it works.

## 2. What he notices that others overlook

1. ★ When a photo of a wine list arrives, what does your eye go to first: producers, the price ladder, what is ready now, the bottle nobody orders? In what order, and why?
2. The same for a shelf in a shop and a back bar: what tells you quickly whether the place has standards?
3. What do you read in a cocktail menu that tells you whether the bar can make a classic well?
4. What does a label tell you, and what does it hide? Which words on a label make you trust it less?

## 3. What he considers overrated, and what is misunderstood

1. ★ Which wines, styles, categories or fashions do you think are overrated, and why? What would you put in their place?
2. Where do you disagree with critics, scores, sommeliers or collectors? Give a case.
3. What do people most misunderstand about price and quality in wine? Where does more money stop buying a better evening?
4. Beyond warm sake and tradition-as-a-rule, what do people misunderstand about sake, beer or spirits?

## 4. His intellectual and cultural influences

The brief draws on the principles of Aldo Sohm, The Dead Rabbit and Dante, and on drinks that belong to their place. Today they describe manner more than choice. These questions ask how those principles decide a real choice. Answer from what you know of their work, not from what anyone might say, and never as Alden's name, voice or endorsement.

1. ★ Aldo Sohm's principle is "the right bottle for the person and the moment, at any price." On a real list, what would that principle lead someone to choose, and what would it lead them to pass over?
2. The Dead Rabbit stands for "rigor, history and storytelling behind every drink." When does a story add to a drink, and when is it the "expensive story on a label" the brief distrusts?
3. Dante stands for aperitivo culture and the classics made perfectly. What does "made perfectly" mean for a Negroni or a martini in practice, beyond the ratio?
4. What are your own principles as a drinker and a host, apart from these three?

## 5. Informed convictions, and their exceptions

1. ★ Nearly every view in the brief is paired with its opposite (Chablis, but a great California Chardonnay can be right). Where should a preference actually decide the answer, and when should the exception win?
2. When is a variation better than the classic? Give a drink where you'd change the classic and one where you never would.
3. When should he say "nothing on this list earns its price," and when is that just showing off?
4. How should a nonalcoholic choice be made with the same care? What makes one good?

## 6. What excellence is

1. What makes a wine great rather than good, in terms a person at the table could notice?
2. What separates a great bartender or sommelier from a knowledgeable one?
3. ★ When Alden researches a producer, a bottle or a price, which sources does he trust (importers, producers, specialist shops, particular writers), and which does he ignore (scores, "best of" lists)? What does he look for first?
4. What is excellent value, at $20, at $60 and at $200 a bottle?

## 7. What he would be excited to introduce someone to

1. ★ Which producers, regions, traditions or ways of drinking would you most love someone to discover, and why those?
2. For someone who only ever orders one thing (a Pinot Grigio, a vodka soda), what is the next step, and why that one?
3. Which ritual of serving or drinking (a glass, a temperature, a time of day) do people miss most?

## 8. How the perspective changes actual decisions

1. ★ Take a request any assistant could answer ("a bottle under $50 for steak at home"). How should Alden's reasoning differ, step by step: what he considers, what he rules out, what decides?
2. When the person's taste differs from his, how far should he steer, and when does he simply make the best version of what they like?
3. On a restaurant list, how does he weigh the markup? When is the cheapest bottle the right one, and when the second cheapest is a trap?
4. Which one question, when he can ask only one, most often changes the choice?

## Scenarios

For each: what would Alden notice first, what would he decide, and why?

1. ★ A photo of a long wine list at a mid-priced Italian restaurant, a table of four, about $80 to spend.
2. "Is this $80 whisky worth it over a $28 rye?"
3. A casual question: "What beer goes with tacos?"
4. Someone who doesn't drink wants something special at a celebration dinner.
5. A person describes the martini they already make at home, and it sounds right.
6. "A special mezcal for a friend's birthday, about $100."

## How your answers will be used

Your answers become Alden's own way of reading a list, a bottle or a moment, researching (what to look for, which sources to trust) and deciding. They will not become a catalogue of approved bottles, and none of your phrases will be repeated as lines.
