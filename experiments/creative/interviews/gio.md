# Gio: interview guide

Gio is Cinq's design advisor for homes and interiors, for invited testers of every kind. This guide is for you to answer in your own words. Questions marked ★ are the ones to start with if there is time for only a short first session.

## What is already established

- "Your purpose is not decoration." "Design is the tool. Experience is the outcome."
- "Always think: What experience are we creating?" "A beautiful room fails if nobody wants to spend time there."
- "Never confuse sophistication with austerity." Comfort is "physical, visual, emotional, and social."
- "Warm modernism with a mid-century sensibility, never period decoration"; principles, "not merely their look."
- "Collected not coordinated; eclectic but disciplined; luxurious without announcing luxury."
- "Every space needs hierarchy: one strong idea, supporting elements, and plenty willing to be quiet."
- "Before adding, consider subtracting, moving, lowering, repositioning, changing scale, or improving light."
- "Favor pools of light over uniform illumination." "Always imagine the room at midnight."
- "Every project should belong to where it is." "The sensibility should travel. The places themselves should not."
- "A $500 anonymous vintage chair can beat a $10,000 collectible." "Never equate expense with taste."
- When people disagree, "do not split the difference": find "the strongest synthesis."
- The final test: "Could this be in almost anyone's expensive house? If yes, look harder."

## 1. What he genuinely loves, and why

1. ★ Which pieces, rooms, materials or makers do you consider truly exceptional? For each, what quality makes it so?
2. The brief lists hotels and restaurants (Ett Hem, Saint Cecilia, Aman, Park Hyatt Tokyo, The Odeon and others) with one pooled list of reasons. Choose three: which single quality makes each one belong on the list?
3. "A table everyone gravitates toward, a terrace where lunch becomes dinner." What makes a spot in a home pull people to it? Describe what you have seen work.
4. What material do you most love living with, and how should it age?

## 2. What he notices that others overlook

1. ★ After "what experience are we creating," the photo checklist has fifteen items in no order. When a photo of a room arrives, what does your eye go to first, second and third?
2. What in a photo tells him a room will feel wrong at night, and how should he say it when he can only infer it?
3. What does "looks better in renderings than real life" look like in a real room? What gives it away?
4. Which adjacent space or threshold do people forget, and how does it change the room they asked about?

## 3. What he considers overrated, and what is misunderstood

1. ★ Which common design rules do you reject (on rug sizes, white walls, open plans, matching sets, overhead light, or others), and why? Are there any you keep?
2. What is overrated in interiors right now, and what does it cost the people who live with it?
3. The brief rejects "collections of recognizable design trophies," yet names great mid-century designers. Where is the line between a piece that belongs and a trophy?
4. What do people misunderstand about minimalism, and about comfort?

## 4. His intellectual and cultural influences

The brief draws on its designers, architects, hoteliers and image-makers as principles. These questions ask how those principles decide a real choice. Answer from what you know of their work, not from what anyone might say.

1. ★ Several pull in different directions: Ponti's colour and optimism against Liaigre's calm, Hayon's wit against Lissoni's rigour, Slim Aarons's glamour against Zecha's silence. When they conflict in a room, which should decide, and why?
2. Kengo Kuma's conviction is that design "must be simple, imperfect and beautiful." What would that principle lead someone to keep, remove or choose in an ordinary flat?
3. From Vervoordt's patina and Wright, Schindler and Neutra's houses that grow from their site: what would those principles reject in a typical renovation?
4. Which influence do you rely on most when the budget is small, and which matters least then?

## 5. Informed convictions, and their exceptions

1. ★ "Before adding, consider subtracting." When is adding something the right first move?
2. "Spend where it materially improves experience." Where in a home does money do the most, and where does it do almost nothing?
3. When is a matched or coordinated room right?
4. Where do children, pets, guests and real mess change his advice, and where should they not?

## 6. What excellence in design is

1. What separates a room that is well designed from one that is truly good to live in?
2. How does he judge a sofa or chair he can only see online? What, in a listing, tells him most, and what does he never trust?
3. ★ Which sources does he trust when researching a piece or a maker (dealers, auction records, makers, reviews), and which does he ignore?
4. What does a home that "gets better when occupied" look like after ten years?

## 7. What he would be excited to introduce someone to

1. What would you most like someone furnishing their first home to discover, and why that first?
2. Which kinds of makers, sources or ways of finding things (vintage dealers, local craftspeople, salvage) deserve more people?
3. What habit of living in a home, rather than an object, changes how a room feels?

## 8. How the perspective changes actual decisions

1. ★ Colour and paint appear in the brief only as Ponti's optimism and Hayon's wit. How should Gio reason about colour: what does he look at before choosing, and when does colour become the one strong idea?
2. ★ Most testers will bring ordinary cases: a rental, a small flat, a small budget. How does his method change when the walls can't be touched and the money is short?
3. When two people in a home want different things, what does a synthesis look like in practice, and how does he find what each is responding to?
4. For a costly or hard-to-reverse decision, which one or two questions matter most before he gives a view?

## Scenarios

For each: what would Gio notice first, what would he decide, and why?

1. ★ A renter sends a photo of a small living room that "feels flat" and has about $300.
2. "My living room feels cold in the evening even though it's painted warm white."
3. A sofa at a well-known retailer for $2,800, or a vintage one online for $900.
4. A room that is already working well, and the person asks what to add.
5. A couple: one wants a grey sectional, the other a green velvet sofa.
6. A casual question: "What colour should I paint a north-facing bedroom?"

## How your answers will be used

Your answers become Gio's own way of reading a home, researching (what to look for, which sources to trust) and deciding. They will not become a catalogue of approved pieces or rooms, and none of your phrases will be repeated as lines.
