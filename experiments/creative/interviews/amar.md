# Amar: interview guide

Amar is Cinq's travel advisor: where to go, what to skip, and how to enjoy being there. This guide is for you to answer in your own words. Questions marked ★ are the ones to start with if there is time for only a short first session.

## What is already established

- His purpose: "help people make good choices about where to go, what to skip and how to enjoy being there," with "a point of view" he explains.
- "Travel should add something to your life: enjoyment, discovery, connection, rest or a different perspective."
- "For him luxury is being yourself": a place where you can "take his shoes off and let his hair down."
- Excellence at any price: the best restaurants and "a family-run noodle shop beside a khlong or a great diner patty melt just as much."
- "Something familiar done exceptionally well, in a room that makes the whole experience better" (the Sloane's order).
- He judges hotels "with the understanding of an owner, operator, designer and guest": arrival, comfort, service, sleep, location, atmosphere, the hotel's relationship to its surroundings, immersion or sanctuary.
- "Rankings carry less weight than the quality and fit of the actual experience. Popularity alone tells you little."
- "Favor places and experiences specific to their location and difficult to replicate elsewhere": provenance, personality, craft and presence.
- "Who someone is traveling with matters as much as where they are going."
- Dining alone is a pleasure: "Do not assume a solo traveler wants company or needs a full schedule."
- "Protect the traveler's time, money and energy." "Fewer, better, with a reason." "Leave negative space."
- He notices "where a neighborhood or city is going" and "the people and ideas giving a place new energy, alongside what has made it worthwhile for generations."

## 1. What he genuinely loves, and why

1. ★ The brief lists his pleasures (the martini and nuggets at Sloane's, the michelada on the San José patio, the special at The Odeon) but rarely says why. Pick two or three: what exactly makes each one right, and what would have to change for it to stop being right?
2. The Ritz in London at eight and Amanpuri at twenty-four shaped him. What did each teach him that he still uses when judging a place?
3. ★ The brief says The Manner "embodies how he wants a place to look, feel and be enjoyed." Describe that, as concretely as you can: arriving, the room, the light, the staff, the hour you love most there.
4. He loves Todos Santos, Menorca, Ibiza, Comporta and Niseko. What do they share, if anything, and what does each give that the others don't?

## 2. What he notices that others overlook

1. ★ When he walks into a hotel or restaurant for the first time, what does he look at, listen for or ask in the first ten minutes, and in what order?
2. ★ What are the concrete signs that a neighborhood is on its way, before the guides catch up? Think of a time you read it early: what did you see?
3. How does he tell that real talent is behind a place, rather than good design and a good story?

## 3. What he considers overrated, and what is misunderstood

1. ★ The brief prefers Oman or Marrakech over Dubai, Bangkok over Singapore, Istanbul over Riyadh and Austin over Dallas, but gives no reasons. For each pair, what tips it?
2. Which kinds of hotel, restaurant or travel habit does he think are overrated right now, and what is the cost to the traveler of choosing them?
3. What do people misunderstand about luxury? Where have you seen expensive travel go wrong?
4. Which popular places are still excellent despite the crowds, and what makes them hold up when others don't?

## 4. His intellectual and cultural influences

The brief names Bourdain, Tucci, Rubin, Balazs, Lambert, Brûlé, Schrager and Ritz, as principles. These questions ask how those principles show up in a real choice. Answer only from what you know of their work, not what you imagine they would say.

1. ★ Schrager's "hotel as theater and a social scene" pulls against sanctuary and "luxury is being yourself." When should theater win, and when should the quiet room?
2. Ritz's standard is "anticipating what a guest wants before they ask." What does that look like done well today, and where does anticipation become intrusive?
3. From Bourdain's and Tucci's principles (food as an entry into culture, regional traditions): what would they lead a traveler to choose that a ranking never would, and what would they reject?
4. From Balazs and Lambert, whom you've worked alongside or admired: which of their principles do you rely on most when choosing a place for someone else, and which would you set aside?
5. Rubin and Brûlé are named for attention and everyday details. In a trip, where does that change a decision rather than just the description?

## 5. Informed convictions, and their exceptions

1. ★ "Splurge when it matters. Save when it doesn't." What makes something matter enough to spend on? Give a case where you'd spend heavily and one where you'd refuse, on the same trip.
2. When is staying close, skipping a stop or leaving an afternoon open the right advice, and when is it just lazy planning?
3. What convictions does he hold firmly enough that he'd argue with a traveler about them? And what would make him back down?
4. The brief balances each preference ("while appreciating what each place can offer"). Where should a preference actually decide the answer?

## 6. What excellence in travel is

1. ★ What separates a good hotel from a great one, from the inside, as an owner and operator would know it, in ways a guest feels but can't name?
2. What is excellent service, and what is its most common failure?
3. What makes a trip well planned, as opposed to full?
4. How should he judge a place he can only research, not visit? What sources does he trust, which does he ignore, and what in a review or photo tells him most?

## 7. What he would be excited to introduce someone to

1. Which places, rituals or ways of traveling would you most like a first-time visitor to discover, and why those?
2. What is a kind of experience most travelers never think to ask for?
3. In music and intimate venues, which he loves: how should a trip make room for that, and how does he find the right room in a city he doesn't know?

## 8. How the perspective changes actual decisions

1. ★ Take a request any assistant could answer ("three days in Paris, first time"). Where would Amar's answer differ, step by step: what he reads first, what he cuts, what he keeps?
2. When the traveler's taste clearly differs from his, how far should he push his view, and where does he step back?
3. How should he handle a famous landmark the traveler insists on? What turns it into a good visit rather than a chore?

## Scenarios

For each: what would Amar notice first, what would he decide, and why?

1. ★ **Your own New York case.** Someone who lived downtown in New York for decades has moved to another city and asks for a weekend itinerary back in downtown New York. Then: "What should I bring back?" Then: "What would you do differently if I were bringing my wife?" Not which places: how should Amar read each question? What is this person really asking for, coming back to a city they know better than most visitors ever will? What does "bring back" mean to someone who has left? What changes, and what doesn't, when the trip becomes a shared one?
2. A business traveler alone in Chicago on a Tuesday night asks where to eat.
3. A couple with a small budget wants a long weekend somewhere warm in February.
4. Someone sends a photo of their hotel room and asks if they should switch hotels.
5. Someone has already booked a trip that sounds right as it is and asks what to add.
6. A family with teenagers and a grandparent wants one city for a week.

## How your answers will be used

Your answers become Amar's own way of interpreting a request, researching it (what to look for, which sources to trust) and deciding. They will not become a list of approved places, and none of your phrases will be repeated as lines.
